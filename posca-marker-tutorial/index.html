What is a POSCA marker?

A quick orientation before you uncap anything.

POSCA markers are water-based pigment paint markers. The ink is opaque, so light colors show up on dark surfaces, and it dries to a matte, water-resistant finish. They work on paper, card, wood, fabric, glass, ceramic, plastic, metal, canvas, stone — almost anything dry and clean.

They use a valve / pump system: paint only flows when you press the nib down. That's why a new marker looks "empty" until you prime it — which is the very first thing we'll do.

Getting started in 4 steps

Do this every time you pick up a marker that hasn't been used in a while.

  1. Shake it (cap on!)

    Shake the marker well for 10–15 seconds. You'll hear a small mixing ball rattle — that stirs the pigment so the color comes out rich and even.

  2. Prime the nib

    Hold the marker upright with the cap off and gently press the nib straight down onto a scrap surface several times. Pump until paint soaks into the tip and the color flows.

  3. Test on scrap paper

    Make a few strokes on scrap before touching your real piece. Wait for a steady, even line — not patchy, not flooding.

  4. Paint in light layers

    Lay down thin, even coats. Let each layer dry, then add another for fully opaque, streak-free color.

Don't over-pump. Too many presses floods the nib and the paint pools or drips. A few gentle pumps is plenty — you can always pump again later.

Choosing the right tip size

POSCA markers come in many nib sizes. Here are the ones beginners reach for most.

ModelLine widthBest for
PC-1MR~0.7 mmFine details, tiny text, outlines
PC-3M0.9–1.3 mmDetail work, lettering, small areas
PC-5M1.8–2.5 mmThe all-rounder — great first marker
PC-8K8 mm chiselBold lines, filling medium areas
PC-17K15 mm broadBackgrounds and large fills
New here? Start with a PC-5M in a few colors plus a PC-3M for details. That pair covers most beginner projects.

Core techniques

The handful of moves that make POSCA work look clean.

Layering

POSCA is opaque, but a single coat can look streaky. Let the first layer dry completely (about a minute), then add a second pass in the same direction for solid, flat color.

Blending

Work fast while the paint is still wet: lay two colors next to each other and feather them together with the nib, or dab with a clean brush. Once it dries, it won't blend anymore.

Outlining & detail

Add fine lines, outlines, and highlights after your base colors are dry. Use a thin nib (PC-1MR / PC-3M) and let dark outlines and white highlights make shapes pop.

Fixing mistakes

On non-porous surfaces (glass, plastic) wet paint wipes away with a damp cloth. On paper, wait for it to dry, then paint over the error — the opaque ink usually hides it.

Your first mini-project

A simple sun-and-mountains scene that uses every technique above.

  1. Sketch lightly

    Pencil in a horizon line, a circle for the sun, and a couple of triangular peaks.

  2. Base colors first

    Fill the sky and sun with the PC-5M. Let it dry, add a second coat for flat color.

  3. Blend the sky

    While the sky is still wet, streak in a warmer color near the horizon and feather it upward.

  4. Add the mountains

    Paint the peaks in a dry layer over the sky. Let them dry fully.

  5. Details last

    Outline the peaks with PC-3M and add white highlights and a few stars. Done!
